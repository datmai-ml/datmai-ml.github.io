Generative Audio for Real-Time Rap

Jan 2025 — May 2026
generative ai transformers audio data pipeline

Research at the Georgia Tech Robotic Musicianship Lab on a system that generates rap vocals in real time, conditioned on lyrics, a backing beat, and a reference voice. The goal was synchronous human-robot rap interaction.

I built the preprocessing pipeline that turned roughly 90,000 raw rap songs into training-ready data. Each song flowed through the same sequence of steps:

Vocal isolation. The source dataset shipped with vocals already separated from the instrumental track for each song. That meant I could derive the instrumental directly by subtracting the vocal waveform from the full mix, avoiding the need to run a source-separation model across the entire dataset.

Chunking on word boundaries. Each song was split into roughly 10-second chunks for training. A naive approach would cut at fixed time intervals, but that risks slicing a word in half and producing garbage training samples. I ran Whisper over each vocal track to get word-level timestamps, then placed chunk boundaries on the nearest natural word break within a 2-second tolerance window. Chunks always end on a fully spoken word.

Encoding to codec tokens. Raw audio is too high-dimensional for a transformer to model directly. A 10-second clip is around 160,000 audio samples. I passed each vocal and accompaniment chunk through XCodec, a neural audio codec that compresses audio into a short sequence of discrete tokens (around 1,600 per 10 seconds) while staying fully decodable back to audio. Generating rap then becomes a sequence-prediction problem over these tokens. The lyrics were embedded with a sentence-transformer model so the rapper could be conditioned on them at training time.

The full pipeline ran on a single GPU. I added aggressive memory cleanup between songs and resumable checkpointing, so a multi-day run could recover from crashes without losing progress and avoid running out of memory across tens of thousands of files.

On the modeling side, I contributed to several major revisions, including the move from MSE to cross-entropy loss after recognizing that codec tokens are discrete classes, and adopting a sliding-window generation approach to reduce discontinuities between audio chunks.

DoReMi Music School

Summer 2025
python sqlite full-stack consulting

A custom desktop application I built for a local music school. The owner was running scheduling, billing, and parent communication out of spreadsheets. Monthly invoicing alone took three to four hours of manual work.

I started with stakeholder interviews to scope the actual workflow. The interviews surfaced details that shaped the design, including the requirement that families with multiple students receive a single combined invoice rather than one per student.

I designed a four-table relational schema in SQLite (families, students, invoices, invoice items), built the desktop application with a Tkinter interface, and integrated PDF generation, Google Drive document storage, and SMS notifications via the Textbelt API.

Monthly invoicing went from 3 to 4 hours down to about 10 minutes. The time savings went into taking on more students.

Diabetic Retinopathy Classifier

Fall 2025
computer vision transfer learning medical imaging

A 5-class severity classifier for diabetic retinopathy, trained on the heavily imbalanced APTOS 2019 retinal-image dataset.

The model uses transfer learning on ResNet50 with strategic layer freezing and aggressive augmentation to limit overfitting on a small medical dataset. Training infrastructure was built in PyTorch Lightning with class-weighted cross-entropy loss to handle the imbalance, ReduceLR scheduling, and custom kappa tracking per epoch.

Achieved a quadratic-weighted kappa above 0.88, which is competitive on this benchmark.

ShinyHunterBot

Early 2026
computer vision cnn automation pokémon

An end-to-end system that automates shiny hunting in Generation 5 Pokémon games (Black, White, Black 2, White 2) running in the DeSmuME emulator. Shinies are rare color variants that appear at a base rate of about 1 in 8,000 encounters.

The bot drives the emulator with simulated keypresses to walk in tall grass and trigger random encounters. Encounter detection runs on a frame-brightness check for the white-fade transition that precedes every battle, confirmed across four consecutive frames to filter out single-frame noise.

The core ML problem is sparkle detection. When a shiny appears in Gen 5, a star-shaped sparkle animation plays over the sprite for a few frames at the start of the encounter. Normal encounters never produce this animation. The sparkle is only visible in a subset of the 60-frame intro, so per-frame classification is noisy. I built a custom CNN that runs over each frame and aggregates predictions across the full 60-frame sequence with a max operation, which corresponds to asking whether any frame in the encounter contained a sparkle.

The data collection layer labels its own training data. The bot toggles between forced-shiny mode using a Gen 5 cheat code and normal mode, producing clean labeled batches from both classes with no manual intervention.
